\begin{abstract}
% The goal of this paper is to impress Marco Túlio Quintino so Anton gets a good grade for his defense. (And a box of chocolate?)
The characterization of possible transformations between quantum states and the quantification of entanglement are central for understanding quantum resources. Explicit results are, however, scarce since they require complicated optimizations over transformation protocols or state decompositions. We introduce a method to optimize functions of the
entanglement spectrum of bipartite states over mixed-state decompositions with arbitrary accuracy. Our results are based on a convergent semidefinite programming hierarchy of optimizations over entanglement spectra using a natural polynomial basis and Schur-Weyl duality. Our approach can also be used
to determine non-polynomial measures, such as the entanglement of formation,
and to characterize the dimensionality of entanglement.
Moreover, extracting the decomposition allows us to formulate
criteria to determine state transformations under local operations
and classical communication (LOCC). In particular,
we provide a hierarchy of certificates of LOCC non-convertibility
from pure to mixed states in any dimension and a heuristic approach
for LOCC convertibility.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
Composite quantum systems are entangled when they cannot be described solely by their parts~\cite{epr}. Operationally, entanglement is a resource for quantum communication~\cite{distributed, eckert} and computation~\cite{one-way, shor} that cannot increase under local operations and classical communications (LOCC) between distant parties.
In isolated two-particle quantum systems, described by pure states, entanglement and LOCC transformations are completely characterized by
the Schmidt coefficients or the eigenvalues of the marginals~\cite{Nielsen1999MajLOCC, Vidal_2000}, also called
the entanglement spectrum. Therefore, learning this spectrum is
necessary and sufficient to understand the entanglement of the
system and the possible entanglement transformations between
states.

In practice, however, pure states become mixed due to decoherence with the environment~\cite{Zurek2003,BreuerPetruccione2002,NielsenChuang2010}. When this occurs, the spectrum of the marginals alone is not informative of the entanglement between them, because a mixed state admits multiple descriptions as a probabilistic ensemble of pure states. Consequently,
one needs to account for all such decompositions. This leads to an intractable optimization required to evaluate entanglement measures~\cite{Bennett1996EoF, Uhlmann1998, Uhlmann2010Roofs, Horodecki2009RMP}, even when full tomography is available.
Existing methods address each specific entanglement measure independently~\cite{Wootters1998, TerhalVollbrecht2000, VollbrechtWerner2001, Mintert2004Concurrence, Chen2005Concurrence, Chen2005EoF, Guhne2007Estimating, Eisert2007QuantWitness}. Moreover, while lower bounds can be found for polynomial measures~\cite{Toth2015CroofTP}, much less is known for the more general case~\cite{PlenioVirmani2007}. As a result, it is still a remarkable challenge to determine when entanglement transformations are possible with LOCC~\cite{Gour2005InfiniteConditions, Chitambar2014LOCC, Sauerwein_2018}.

Here we aim to provide a systematic method to learn the entanglement properties of mixed bipartite quantum states.
To this end we use the Schur-Weyl decomposition of the Hilbert space of multiple copies, which allows one
to learn entanglement spectra of pure states \cite{Alicki1988, Keyl2001EstimatingSpectrum} to estimate any polynomial entanglement measure for mixed states with a complete polynomial basis. This basis is particularly suited to detect entanglement dimensionality. We find upper and lower converging bounds, which determine, for instance, the amount of entanglement of the main families of bound entangled states. Using semidefinite and linear programming, we extend them to a convergent hierarchy to find any possible entanglement measure of mixed states.
The combination of these bounds leads us to a hierarchy of certificates to determine LOCC convertibility from pure to mixed and a heuristic approach towards convertibility.

\section{Entanglement spectrum}

Consider a bipartite pure state $\ket{\psi}_{AB} \in \C^{d_A} \otimes \C^{d_B} \cong \C^D$ with $D = d_Ad_B$. Such a state is separable if it can be written as $\ket{\psi}=\ket{a}\otimes\ket{b}$ and entangled otherwise. More generally, any pure bipartite state admits a \emph{Schmidt decomposition}
\begin{equation}\label{eq:SchmidtDec}
    \ket{\psi}_{AB} = \sum_{i=1}^r \sqrt{\a_i}\ket{a_i}\otimes\ket{b_i},
\end{equation}
where $\a_1\geq\a_2\geq\dots\geq\a_r>0$ and the sets $\{\ket{a_i}\}_i$ and $\{\ket{b_i}\}_i$ are orthonormal. The spectrum $\a=(\a_1,\dots,\a_{d_A})$ of the reduced density matrix $\rho_A=\tr_B\dyad{\psi}$ (with $\a_i=0$ for $i>r$) is called the {\em entanglement spectrum} of $\ket{\psi}_{AB}$, and the number $r$ of nonzero coefficients is known as the {\em Schmidt rank}, denoted $\mathrm{SR}(\psi)$.

Every measure below can be evaluated on it.}
By the Nielsen theorem~\cite{Nielsen1999MajLOCC}, a bipartite pure state $\ket{\psi}$ with entanglement spectrum $\a(\psi)$ can be converted by LOCC into another bipartite pure state $\ket{\phi}$ with entanglement spectrum $\a(\phi)$ if and only if $E_j(\psi)\geq E_j(\phi)$ for all $j=1,\dots,d_A-1$, where
\begin{equation}\label{eq:Vidal}
    E_j(\psi)=1-\sum_{i=1}^{j} \a_i(\psi),
\end{equation}
as defined in Ref.~\cite{Vidal_1999}. In this sense, the quantities $E_j(\psi)$ form a complete set of entanglement monotones, and all information about the entanglement of each state is encoded in the entanglement spectrum. Every pure-state entanglement monotone is a concave, unitarily invariant function of $\rho_A$~\cite{Vidal_2000}.

\section{Decomposition of the Hilbert space}
Let us now explain facts about the Hilbert space of $k$ copies of a state $\ket{\psi}$, since this is the basis of our approach.
A general $k$-qudit quantum state is a vector in the Hilbert space $\cdk$. We consider two groups acting on this space: the \emph{symmetric group} $S_k$ of permutations $\pi\in S_k$ of $k$ elements, acting as
\begin{align}
    \pi (\ket{\psi_1} \ot \dots \ot \ket{\psi_k}) &= \ket{\psi_{\pi^{-1}(1)}} \ot \dots \ot \ket{\psi_{\pi^{-1}(k)}},
\end{align}
and the \emph{general linear group} $GL_d$ of invertible $d\times d$ matrices $g\in GL_d$, acting as
\begin{align}
g(\ket{\psi_1} \ot \dots \ot \ket{\psi_k}) &=g\ket{\psi_1} \ot \dots \ot g\ket{\psi_k}.
\end{align}
These two actions commute,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:PerUniCommute}
    \pi \, g = g \, \pi.
\end{equation}
The so-called \emph{Schur-Weyl duality}~\cite{fulton2013representation} states that under the action of these two groups, the Hilbert space $\cdk$ decomposes as
\begin{equation}
    \cdk = \bigoplus_{\substack{\lambda\vdash k \\ \ell(\lambda)\leq d}} \mathcal{S}^\lambda_k \ot \mathcal{U}^\lambda_d
\end{equation}
into irreducible subspaces $\mathcal{S}^\lambda_k$ and $\mathcal{U}^\lambda_d$, which carry the irreducible representations
of $S_k$ and $GL_d$, respectively, indexed by partitions $\lambda$. The dimensions of $\mathcal{S}^\lambda_k$ and $\mathcal{U}^\lambda_d$ are denoted as $f^\lambda$ and $s_\lambda(1,\dots,1)$, where $s_\lambda$ is the so-called Schur polynomial (see also below). Each partition $\lambda$ is represented as a Young diagram: a non-increasing vector of positive integers summing to $k$, whose length we denote by $\ell(\lambda)$.  We write $\lambda \vdash k$ to denote all the partitions $\lambda$ of $k$.

To each Young diagram $\lambda$ we can associate a so-called {\em isotypic projector} $\Pi^\lambda$ onto the subspace $\mathcal{S}^\lambda_k \ot \mathcal{U}^\lambda_d$. This projector is given by
\begin{align}
    \Pi^\lambda = \frac{f^\lambda}{k!}\sum_{\pi \in S_k} \chi^\lambda(\pi)\, \pi,
\end{align}
where $\chi^\lambda(\pi)$ is the character of the cycle type of $\pi$ in the irreducible representation $\lambda$. It follows that the isotypic projectors commute with both permutations and tensor powers of matrices,
\begin{equation}
    \pi\,\Pi^\lambda = \Pi^\lambda\,\pi\,,\quad g\,\Pi^\lambda = \Pi^\lambda\,g\,.
\end{equation}

\section{Polynomial optimization over mixed-state decompositions}
We are now ready to present the main tool that we use to address the problem of entanglement estimation and convertibility certification. The spectrum of a reduced state $\rho_A$ can be estimated via weak Schur sampling on $k$ copies of $\rho_A$~\cite{Alicki1988,Keyl2001EstimatingSpectrum}: the probability of measuring a Young diagram $\lambda \vdash k$ is given by
\begin{equation}
    \tr(\Pi^\lambda \rho_A^{\otimes k}) = f^\lambda s_\lambda(\spec\rho_A).
\end{equation}
It is known that the Schur polynomials $s_\lambda$ with $\lambda \vdash k$ and $\ell(\lambda) \leq d_A$ form a basis of the homogeneous symmetric polynomials $P$ of degree $k$ of the spectrum of $\rho_A$ \cite{MacdonaldSymmFunc}, and they expand into the monomial basis in the following way:
\begin{equation}
    s_\lambda(\spec\rho_A) = \sum_{\mu \vdash k} K_{\lambda\mu}m_\mu(\spec\rho_A),
\end{equation}
where $m_\mu$ is the monomial symmetric polynomial
\begin{equation}
    m_\mu(\a) = \sum_{\beta} \a_1^{\beta_1}\a_2^{\beta_2}\dots\a_{d_A}^{\beta_{d_A}}
\end{equation}
where the sum runs over all {\em distinct} permutations $\beta$ of $(\mu_1, \mu_2, \dots, \mu_{\ell(\mu)}, 0,\dots, 0) \in \N^{d_A}$, and $K_{\lambda\mu}$ is a constant known as the Kostka number~\cite{MacdonaldSymmFunc}.

Since $\tr(\rho_A) = 1$ implies $\tr(\rho_A)^{l} = 1$, any polynomial of degree $l < k$ can be lifted to a homogeneous polynomial of degree $k$. Thus, any symmetric polynomial of degree at most $k$ of the entanglement spectrum can be expressed as
\begin{equation}
    P(\spec\rho_A) = \sum_{\lambda\vdash k}c_\lambda s_\lambda(\spec\rho_A).
\end{equation}

In order to estimate any entanglement measure of bipartite states given by a symmetric polynomial, we are interested in the convex roof of these polynomials,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:PrhoABcroof}
    \widehat{P}(\rho_{AB}) = \min_{\mathcal{D}(\rho_{AB})} \sum_i p_i \tr(O_P\, \rho_{A,i}^{\otimes k}),
\end{equation}
where $\rho_{A,i}=\tr_B\dyad{\psi_i}$ and
\begin{equation}\label{eq:OP}
    O_P = \sum_{\lambda \vdash k} \frac{c_\lambda}{f^\lambda} \Pi^\lambda
\end{equation}
for any tuple of coefficients $\vec{c}:=(c_{\lambda_1},\dots,c_{\lambda_t})$, where $t$ is the number of partitions of $k$. $\widehat{P}$ is an entanglement monotone if $P$ is concave~\cite{Vidal_2000}. For any $P$, we always have unitary invariance since $P$ only depends on the spectrum.

\section{A complete hierarchy for entanglement estimation}
Our approach consists of using the spectrum estimation formulation above for mixed-state decompositions. To this end, we use that polynomial entanglement measures can be bounded using
optimizations over functions of several copies of a state~\cite{Toth2015CroofTP}.
That is, given a bipartite mixed state $\rho_{AB}$ acting on $\C^{d_A} \otimes \C^{d_B}$, to any decomposition in $\mathcal{D}(\rho_{AB})$ we associate the state
\begin{equation}\label{eq:omega}
    \omega_k = \sum_i p_i \dyad{\psi_i}^{\otimes k}
\end{equation}
acting on $(\C^{d_A} \otimes \C^{d_B})^{\otimes k}$.
The core idea is that a polynomial entanglement measure of degree not larger than $k$ can be written as a linear function on $\omega_k$. Then, optimizing over all decompositions amounts to optimizing over all $\omega_k$ with
the constraint that $\omega_k$ is symmetric and fully separable with respect to the $k$ copies. The separability constraint is difficult to implement, but it can be treated by a hierarchy of semidefinite approximations, similar to the separability criterion of symmetric extensions
\cite{doherty2002}.

When $\deg(P) = m < k$, $P$ can be lifted to degree $k$, or, equivalently, evaluated on $A_1,\dots,A_m$ as both give the same bound (Lemma~\ref{lem:lifting}). $\Pi_\text{sym}^{AB,k}$ is the projector onto the $k$-copy symmetric subspace of $(\C^{d_A}\otimes\C^{d_B})^{\otimes k}$ across copies, and $T_l$ denotes the partial transpose on the first $l$ copies. Since every $\omega_k$ is in the feasible set of this SDP at every level $k$, it gives a lower bound on the convex roof. This yields an SDP hierarchy of $k$-th order lower bounds. \section{Simplified relaxation}

Notice that the isotypic projectors $\Pi^\lambda$ only act on Alice's subsystem copies. This leads us to ask whether the SDP can be simplified. In turn, here we present a simplification of the hierarchy above using a smaller variable that allows us to access larger systems, at the cost of weaker bounds at each level $P_{{\rm LB}_{k,m}} \leq P_{{\rm LB}_m}$ with $P_{{\rm LB}_{k,m}}$ defined in Eq.~\eqref{eq:marginals_SDP} below. For $m \geq k$, define the following extension acting on $(\C^{d_A})^{\otimes m}\otimes(\C^{d_B})^{\otimes k}$ (i.e., $m$ copies of $A$ and $k$ copies of $B$),
\begin{equation}\label{eq:omega_km}
    \omega_{k,m} := \sum_i p_i \dyad{\psi_i}^{\otimes k}\otimes \rho_{A,i}^{\otimes (m-k)}
\end{equation}
with $\rho_{A,i} = \tr_B(\dyad{\psi_i})$. We relax this operator with a positive semidefinite variable $\Phi_{k,m}$, and denote $\Phi_{k}:=\tr_{A_{k+1}, \dots, A_m}(\Phi_{k,m})$.
Then we can lower bound $\widehat{P}(\rho)$ as $P_{{\rm LB}_{k,m}}\leq \widehat{P}(\rho)$, where
\begin{align}\label{eq:marginals_SDP}
    P_{{\rm LB}_{k,m}}:=&\min_{\Phi_{k,m}} \tr(O_P^A \otimes \I^B \ \Phi_{k,m})\\
    \text{s.t. } & \Phi_{k,m}\succeq 0, \nonumber\\
    & \Pi_\text{sym}^{AB,k} \, \Phi_{k}\, \Pi_\text{sym}^{AB,k}= \Phi_{k},\nonumber\\
    & \tr_{A_2,\dots,A_m,B_2,\dots,B_k} (\Phi_{k,m}) = \rho_{AB}, \nonumber\\
    & \tr_{B_1,\dots,B_k}(\Phi_{k,m}) \text{ is permutation invariant},\nonumber\\
    & \Phi_{k,m}^{T_S} \succeq 0 \quad  \forall \, S.\nonumber
\end{align}
Here $O_P^A$ is the observable of Eq.~\eqref{eq:OP} for a polynomial of degree $m$, acting on $A_1,\dots,A_m$, and $T_S$ denotes the partial transpose on a set $S$ of registers, where transposing a full copy acts on both $A_i$ and $B_i$. Since $\Phi_{k,m}$ can be assumed invariant under permutations of $A_1B_1, \dots, A_kB_k$ and $A_{k+1}, \dots, A_m$, it suffices to impose partial transpose conditions on the tuples $(i, j)$ where each tuple means taking the partial transpose on the first $i$ full-copy registers and the first $j$ marginal registers. One such set of representatives is $\{(i,j):0\leq i\leq k,\ 0\leq j<\tfrac{m-k}{2}\}\ \cup\ \{(i,\tfrac{m-k}{2}):0\leq i\leq\lfloor k/2\rfloor\}$ where the second set is only present for even $m-k$ and the tuple $(0,0)$ is excluded.
For $k\geq\deg P$, the marginal $\Phi_k$ of any feasible $\Phi_{k,m}$ is feasible for Eq.~\eqref{eq:SDPcroof1} at level $k$, so that $P_{{\rm LB}_k}\leq P_{{\rm LB}_{k,m}}$ and this hierarchy is also complete in $k$. In fact, notice that for $k=m$ the hierarchy of Eq.~\eqref{eq:marginals_SDP} reduces to Eq.~\eqref{eq:SDPcroof1}.

\section{Approximating the optimal decomposition}\label{sec:extractingTheDecomposition}
Our next goal is to extract the decomposition $\{p_i, \ket{\psi_i}\}$ from the optimal solution $\Omega_k$ found in the SDP. This will provide us control of how tight our estimates are. Notice that if the relaxation is tight, we have $\Omega_k = \omega_k$. This is in general not the case, and therefore we write $\Omega_k = \omega_k + \Delta$, where $\Delta$ accounts for the gap of the relaxation and solver noise.
For $k \geq 3$, this decomposition is unique up to phases when the operators $\dyad{\psi_i}$ are linearly independent~\cite{Kruskal1977}. When the relaxation is tight and $\Omega_k = \omega_k$, we can then retrieve it exactly with Jennrich's algorithm~\cite{Harshman1970,Leurgans1993}. Since $\Omega_k$ is in general not of this form, we instead use the PARAFAC (or CANDECOMP) algorithm~\cite{Harshman1970,CarrollChang1970} from the TensorLy package~\cite{tensorly}, initialized with Jennrich's algorithm, to find a decomposition $\{p_j, \ket{\psi_j}\}$ that locally minimizes the Frobenius norm
\begin{equation}
    \Theta_k:=\Big\|\Omega_k - \sum_j p_j \dyad{\psi_j}^{\otimes k}\Big\|_F,
\end{equation}
with $\|X\|_F = \sqrt{\sum_{i,j} |X_{ij}|^2}$. Details are given in Appendix~\ref{app:tensor}.
\detail{Tensor decompositions are new to most physics readers. Add background: CP rank; why matrix decompositions are not unique but order-3 tensor decompositions are (Kruskal's condition with $k$-ranks); Jennrich's algorithm step by step (two random contractions, simultaneous diagonalisation, read off the factors); alternating least squares for PARAFAC. A toy example (two qubits, a three-term decomposition recovered exactly from $\omega_3$) shows the method working before the noisy case.}
We obtain a decomposition of the state $\sigma = \sum_j p_j \dyad{\psi_j}$, which is close to, but generally different from $\rho$. We turn it into an exact decomposition of $\rho$ by defining
\begin{equation}\label{eq:repair}
    \ket{\varphi_j} = \rho^{1/2}\sigma^{-1/2}\sqrt{p_j}\ket{\psi_j},
\end{equation}
where $\sigma^{-1/2}$ is taken on the support of $\sigma$. If $\operatorname{supp} \rho \subseteq \operatorname{supp} \sigma$, then $\sum_j \dyad{\varphi_j} = \rho^{1/2}\Pi_\sigma\rho^{1/2} = \rho$, with $\Pi_\sigma$ the projector onto the support of $\sigma$. Hence $\{q_j,\ket{\phi_j}\}$ with $q_j = \|\varphi_j\|^2$ and $\ket{\phi_j}=\ket{\varphi_j}/\|\varphi_j\|$ is a decomposition of $\rho$ and gives a valid upper bound on the convex roof.

\section{Non-polynomial measures via Linear Programming}\label{sec:LP}

So far we have introduced a method to estimate polynomial entanglement measures, by using a natural basis of symmetric polynomials in the entanglement spectrum. Here we aim to use this approach to estimate non-polynomial entanglement measures. To this end notice that all continuous entanglement measures can be bounded arbitrarily well by infinite series of polynomials. Our goal is therefore to find the polynomial that best approximates the target measure. Namely, we aim to find
\begin{equation}\label{eq:FLBexact}
    \widetilde{F}_{{\rm LB}_k}(\rho)=\sup_{\substack{P\leq F\\ \deg P\leq k}}\ \min_{\Omega_k}\ \tr\left((O_P^A\otimes\I^B)\,\Omega_k\right),
\end{equation}
the optimization over symmetric polynomials of degree at most $k$ that lower bound $F$ on all spectra.

Using that all such polynomials can be written in the (discrete) basis of Schur polynomials, we will show that finding the best polynomial that lower bounds any entanglement measure is a problem that can be cast as a linear program (LP) over the coefficients in this basis. This allows us to dynamically find the best lower-bounding polynomial for a given state while running the SDP.

The proof is constructive, namely we will present how to construct such LP. First, we select multiple spectra $\a=(\a_1, \dots, \a_{d_A})$ at which we compute all the Schur polynomials $s_\lambda(\a)$ of degree $k$ and the input function $F(\a)$. These are points of the simplex of possible spectra $\alpha$, and define a grid $G=\{\a^g\}$ discretizing this simplex of possible spectra (Fig.~\ref{fig:grid}). Here the superindex $\a^g$ is chosen to distinguish the grid point $g$ from the subindex $j$ denoting each corresponding eigenvalue $\a^g_j$. We then require $F(\a^g) \geq \sum_\lambda c_\lambda s_\lambda(\a^g)$ at every spectrum $\a^g\in G$. By symmetry of the Schur polynomials, we only need to compute the points where $\a_i \geq \a_j$ for $i < j$ for each spectrum $\a$.
Thus, we are left with the following optimization problem,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:FLBgrid}
    F_{{\rm LB}_k}(\rho)=\max_{\substack{P(\a^g)\leq F(\a^g)\,\forall g\\ \deg P\leq k}}\ \min_{\Omega_k}\ \tr\left((O_P^A\otimes\I^B)\,\Omega_k\right).
\end{equation}

In order to solve this optimization, we need to address two challenges. First, a certain error $\epsilon_k$ is inherent to this grid procedure, in the sense that $|F_{{\rm LB}_k}-\widetilde{F}_{{\rm LB}_k}|\leq \epsilon_k$ (Fig.~\ref{fig:grid}).
Since we can retrieve the polynomial as output from the SDP, we can compute this error numerically and subtract it from the output to obtain a lower bound, namely $\widetilde{F}_{{\rm LB}_k}\geq F_{{\rm LB}_k} - \epsilon_k$. Second, this is in principle a min-max optimization problem. However, since the LP is feasible and has a bounded solution, strong duality holds, and we can turn this problem into a single minimization problem by taking the dual of the LP. Details are given in App.~\ref{app:LP}.

\section{LOCC convertibility}
Finally, let us demonstrate how the presented approach allows us to determine whether certain pairs of states can be transformed by means of LOCC. To date, and to the best of our knowledge, the only criteria known for this problem are restricted to qubits and very special families of states~\cite{Chitambar2014LOCC,Gour2005InfiniteConditions}.

To this end, let us first recall the following criterion~\cite{Jonathan_1999}: a pure state $\ket{\psi}$ can be converted into an ensemble $\{p_i, \ket{\psi_i}\}$ by LOCC, if and only if
\begin{equation}\label{eq:JPcriterion}
    E_j(\psi) \geq \sum_i p_i E_j(\psi_i)\qquad \text{for all } j=1,\dots,d_A-1,
\end{equation}
where $E_j$ are the Vidal monotones~\eqref{eq:Vidal}.
Hence, $\ket{\psi}$ can be converted into a mixed state $\rho$ if and only if some decomposition of $\rho$ satisfies all these inequalities simultaneously.
\detail{Explain the Jonathan--Plenio criterion: what converting a pure state into an ensemble means (local operations with flagged outcomes), why it reduces to the Vidal monotones, and why converting into $\rho$ only needs \emph{some} decomposition (the flags are forgotten). A $2\times2$ example where the answer is known lets the reader check the method.}

Notice that this is not straightforward to verify, since the decompositions of $\rho$ maximizing each monotone $E_j$ might be different. Therefore, to assure that all inequalities hold for the same decomposition (even if not optimal), we combine the LPs of Sec.~\ref{sec:LP} for all the $E_j$ into a single SDP. With a grid of spectra $\{\a^g\}_g$, we define the following optimization:
\begin{align}\label{eq:LOCCminmaxt}
    s_k=\min_{\Omega_k} \max_t \max_{P_j\in\mathcal{P}_j}\ \sum_j t_j\Big(\tr\left((O_{P_j}\otimes\I^B)\Omega_k\right)-E_j(\psi)\Big),
\end{align}
where $t$ is a probability vector and $\mathcal{P}_j$ is the set of polynomials of degree $k$ satisfying the grid constraint.
\detail{Give the SDP form (Eq.~\eqref{eq:LOCCsdp}) in the main text as well as the min--max form, and explain in words why the weights $t_j$ appear: the certificate is a convex combination of the violated inequalities. Mention that this SDP was infeasible for pure and low-rank targets until the polynomial coefficients were bounded ($|c|\le100$), and that it uses a fixed $1/60$ lattice because cutting planes were too slow there.}

The optimal value $s_k$ is the smallest worst-case violation of Eq.~\eqref{eq:JPcriterion} over the set of relaxations of possible decompositions. The dual variables of this SDP provide weights $t_j^*\geq0$ with $\sum_jt^*_j=1$ and polynomials $Q_j^*$ satisfying $Q^*_j\leq t^*_jE_j$ on the grid (Appendix~\ref{app:LOCC}).
As shown in Sec.~\ref{sec:extractingTheDecomposition}, we can extract the decomposition from the result of the SDP. This leads us to a certificate that certain pure-to-mixed LOCC transformations are possible via Eq.~\eqref{eq:JPcriterion}. This can be summarized as follows.

\begin{result}
By extracting a decomposition $\{p_i, \ket{\psi_i}\}$, we can verify whether it satisfies Eq.~\eqref{eq:JPcriterion} for a certain input state $\ket{\psi}$, and therefore determine whether they can be transformed with LOCC.
\end{result}
However, unlike for the bounds for polynomial and non-polynomial measures, it may not always be possible to extract the correct decomposition. This comes from the fact that there may not exist a decomposition of $\rho$ into linearly independent terms that satisfies the criterion of Eq.~\eqref{eq:JPcriterion}, which makes it very difficult for the algorithms of Sec.~\ref{sec:extractingTheDecomposition} to find the underlying decomposition.
Instead, a similar strategy allows us to obtain a complete no-go criterion to discard entanglement transformations.

\begin{result}\label{thm:LOCC}
We obtain a complete hierarchy for LOCC non-convertibility in the following sense:
    \begin{enumerate}
    \item Let $\epsilon_j=\max_{\a^g}\left(Q^*_j(\a^g)-t^*_jE_j(\a^g)\right)$ be the grid errors of the polynomials returned by the solver.
    If $s_k>\sum_j\epsilon_j$ for some $k$, then $\ket{\psi}$ cannot be converted into $\rho$ by LOCC.
    \item In the limit where the grid $G$ is continuous and covers all possible spectra $\alpha$, for any $\ket{\psi}$ and $\rho$, if $\ket{\psi} \overset{\text{LOCC}}{\nrightarrow} \rho$, there is a finite level $k$ at which $s_k > 0$.
    \end{enumerate}
\end{result}
The proof is given in Appendix~\ref{app:LOCC}. Notice that although the grid will always be discrete in practical computations, by continuity the second item suggests that finer grids lead to tighter criteria. Numerically, we verify that this is the case for all states tested.

\section{Conclusions}

Using spectral estimation techniques and semidefinite programming (SDP), we have introduced a method to estimate any entanglement measure for bipartite mixed states.
This method also allows us to address the problem of determining whether entanglement transformations are possible from pure to mixed states, with a hierarchy of certificates of non-convertibility and a heuristic approach for convertibility.
This addresses a major barrier at certifying  quantum resources and their convertibility, and demonstrates the applicability of Schur-Weyl duality in this area.

For future research, it would be central to extend our approach to the multipartite case, where not all entanglement monotones are determined by the spectrum of the
reduced states. The classification of convertibility between multipartite states
is extremely difficult~\cite{Sauerwein_2018}, but at the same time highly relevant.
The reason is that relevant quantum phenomena like magic (for quantum computation) or the Fisher information (for quantum metrology) are inherently linked to multipartite systems. Extending our approach to these scenarios could be an essential step towards a full understanding of quantum resources.

\section{Proof of Result~\ref{thm:ConvergenceSDPcroof}}\label{app:ProofCongNonExtHier}
We consider a polynomial $P$ of degree $m$. At level $k \geq m$ of the hierarchy, we obtain the optimal solution $\Omega_k$, with $P_{{\rm LB}_k}(\rho) = \tr(O_P\, \Omega_m)$ where $\Omega_m = \tr_{m+1,\dots, k}(\Omega_k)$ is the $m$-copy marginal of $\Omega_k$.
By the de Finetti theorem for Bose-symmetric states~\cite[Thm.~II.8]{Christandl_2007}, for every feasible $\Omega_k$, there is an $\omega_m = \int \dyad{\phi}^{\otimes m} d\mu(\phi)$ such that
\begin{equation}
    \left\|\Omega_m - \omega_m\right\|_1 \leq \epsilon
\end{equation}
with $\epsilon = \tfrac{4 D m}{k}$. By Carath\'eodory's theorem, since the space is finite-dimensional, we can write $\omega_m = \sum_i p_i \dyad{\phi_i}^{\otimes m}$. Here, $\omega_m$ is associated to a decomposition $\{p_i, \ket{\phi_i}\}_i$ corresponding to a state $\sigma$ and $\tr(O_P \,\omega_m) \geq \widehat{P}(\sigma)$. Since the partial trace is contractive, $\left\|\rho - \sigma\right\|_1 = \left\|\tr_{2,\dots, m}(\Omega_m - \omega_m)\right\|_1 \leq \epsilon$.

Then, by H\"{o}lder inequality \eqref{eq:HolderStep}, by definition of the convex roof \eqref{eq:croofstep} and from the continuity of the convex roof of a continuous function in finite dimensions \eqref{eq:contConv}~\cite[Thm.~6.1]{Bucicovschi2013},
\begin{align}
    \tr(O_P \Omega_m) &\geq \tr(O_P\omega_m) - \epsilon\|O_P\|_\infty \label{eq:HolderStep}\\
    &\geq \widehat{P}(\sigma) - \epsilon\|O_P\|_\infty \label{eq:croofstep}\\
    &\geq \widehat{P}(\rho) - \delta(\epsilon) - \epsilon\|O_P\|_\infty \label{eq:contConv}
\end{align}
with $\delta(\epsilon) \to 0$.

\section{Lifting}
\begin{lemma}\label{lem:lifting}
    Let $P$ be a homogeneous symmetric polynomial of degree $m$ with $m < k$. Let $P_k$ be its lift to degree $k$ such that $P_k(\a) = P(\a) \left(\sum_i \a_i \right)^{k-m} = P(\a)$ where $\a$ is the spectrum of a state $\rho_A$.
    Then, for every $\Omega_k$ acting on $(\C^{d_A} \otimes \C^{d_B})^{\otimes k}$, which is permutation invariant across all $k$ copies of $\C^{d_A} \otimes \C^{d_B}$,
    \begin{equation}
        \tr(O_P^{A_1,\dots, A_m} \otimes \I^{A_{m+1}, \dots, A_k} \otimes \I^{B} \Omega_k) = \tr(O_{P_k}^A \otimes \I^B \Omega_k).
    \end{equation}
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
    Let $\mathcal{T} = \frac{1}{k!}\sum_{\pi \in S_k} \pi \ O_P \otimes \I^{\otimes (k-m)} \ \pi^\dagger$ be the twirl of $O_P \otimes \I^{\otimes (k-m)}$ over $S_k$. Obviously $\mathcal{T}$ commutes with every permutation in $S_k$, and, since it is a linear combination of permutations, we can write it as a sum of isotypic projectors $\mathcal{T} = \sum_{\lambda \vdash k} a_\lambda \Pi^\lambda$ for some coefficients $a_\lambda$.

    For any $\rho_A$ with spectrum $\a$, since $\rho_A^{\otimes k}$ is permutation invariant,
    \begin{equation}
        \tr(\mathcal{T}\rho_A^{\otimes k}) = \tr(\frac{1}{k!} \sum_{\pi \in S_k} \pi O_P \otimes \I \pi^\dagger \rho_A^{\otimes k})= \tr(O_P \otimes \I \rho_A^{\otimes k}).
    \end{equation}
    Thus $\mathcal{T}$ and $O_{P_k}$ have the same expectation value $P_k(\a)$ on every $\rho_A^{\otimes k}$, and since both are combinations of isotypic projectors and the Schur polynomials are linearly independent, $\mathcal{T} = O_{P_k}$.
    Finally,
    \begin{align}
        &\tr(O_P^{A_1,\dots, A_m} \otimes \I^{A_{m+1}, \dots, A_k} \otimes \I^{B} \Omega_k)\\
        =& \tr( \frac{1}{k!}\sum_{\pi \in S_k} O_P^{A_1,\dots, A_m} \otimes \I^{A_{m+1}, \dots, A_k} \otimes \I^{B} \pi^\dagger \Omega_k \pi)\\
        =& \tr( \frac{1}{k!}\sum_{\pi \in S_k}  \pi O_P^{A_1,\dots, A_m} \otimes \I^{A_{m+1}, \dots, A_k} \otimes \I^{B} \pi^\dagger \Omega_k)\\
        =& \tr( O_{P_k} \otimes \I^{B} \Omega_k)
    \end{align}
\end{proof}

\section{Symmetry reductions and optimizations}\label{app:SymRed}
Throughout this appendix, a single copy of $\C^{d_A}\otimes\C^{d_B}$ is identified with $\C^D$, with orthonormal basis $\{\ket{j}\}_{j=0}^{D-1}$.

\subsection{Restriction to the symmetric subspace}
The symmetric subspace $\text{Sym}^k(\C^D)$ has an orthonormal basis indexed by occupation vectors. The occupation vector denoted $\nu(i_1,\dots,i_k)\in\N^D$ of a basis vector $\ket{i_1,\dots,i_k}\in(\C^D)^{\otimes k}$ has entries $\nu_j$ equal to the number of times $j$ appears in $(i_1,\dots,i_k)$. For example, $\nu(0,1,2,0,0,1,2,2)=(3,2,3)$ for $D=3$, $k=8$. We define the isometry $V_k:(\C^D)^{\otimes k}\to\text{Sym}^k(\C^D)$ as
\begin{equation}
    V_k = \sum_{i_1, \dots, i_k=0}^{D-1} \binom{k}{\nu(i_1, \dots, i_k)}^{-1/2}\ket{\nu(i_1, \dots, i_k)}\bra{i_1, \dots, i_k},
\end{equation}
where $\binom{k}{\nu}=k!/\prod_j\nu_j!$ is the multinomial coefficient, i.e., the number of basis vectors with occupation vector $\nu$. It has real entries and satisfies $V_kV_k^\dagger=\I_{\text{Sym}^k}$ and $V_k^\dagger V_k=\Pi_{\rm sym}^{k}$. Since every feasible $\Omega_k$ is supported on the symmetric subspace, $\Omega_k=V_k^\dagger\Omega_k^{\rm sym}V_k$ with $\Omega_k^{\rm sym}=V_k\Omega_kV_k^\dagger$, a matrix of size $\binom{k+D-1}{k}$. The objective becomes $\tr\left(V_k(O_P\otimes\I^B)V_k^\dagger\,\Omega_k^{\rm sym}\right)$.

\subsection{First marginal constraint}
Here we wish to encode the condition $\tr_{A_2B_2\dots A_kB_k}(\Omega_k) = \rho$.
We define the occupation vector $\nu_\downarrow$ such that $\ket{\nu_\downarrow} \in \text{Sym}^{k-1}(\C^D)$, and $\ket{\nu_\downarrow + e_j} \in \text{Sym}^k(\C^D)$ has one more element $\ket{j}$, i.e., $(\nu_\downarrow + e_j)_j=(\nu_\downarrow)_j + 1$. Grouping the $\binom{k-1}{\nu_\downarrow}$ tuples $(i_2,\dots,i_k)$ with the same occupation vector and using $\binom{k}{\nu_\downarrow+e_j}=\binom{k-1}{\nu_\downarrow}\frac{k}{\nu_{\downarrow,j}+1}$, we get
\begin{equation}
    \left(\tr_{2,\dots,k}\Omega_k\right)_{jj'}
    =\sum_{\nu_\downarrow}\binom{k-1}{\nu_\downarrow}\frac{\bra{\nu_\downarrow+e_j}\Omega_k^{\rm sym}\ket{\nu_\downarrow+e_{j'}}}{\sqrt{\binom{k}{\nu_\downarrow+e_j}\binom{k}{\nu_\downarrow+e_{j'}}}}
    =\frac1k\tr\!\left(\hat a_{j'}^\dagger\hat a_j\,\Omega_k^{\rm sym}\right),
\end{equation}
with the bosonic creation operators $\hat a_j^\dagger:\text{Sym}^{k-1}(\C^D)\to\text{Sym}^k(\C^D)$,
\begin{equation}
    \hat a_j^\dagger=\sum_{\nu_\downarrow}\sqrt{\nu_{\downarrow,j}+1}\,\ket{\nu_\downarrow+e_j}\bra{\nu_\downarrow}.
\end{equation}
Thus, the marginal constraint becomes the linear constraint $\frac1k\sum_{j,j'}\tr\left(\hat a_{j'}^\dagger\hat a_j\,\Omega_k^{\rm sym}\right)\ket{j}\bra{j'}=\rho$.

\subsection{Partial-transpose constraints}
Thanks to the permutation symmetry, the partial transpose only depends on the size of the set of transposed copies, and since $\Omega_k^{T_{S^c}}=(\Omega_k^{T_S})^T$, a set and its complement give equivalent constraints. We therefore impose the PPT constraints on the first $l$ copies for $l \in \{1, \dots, \lfloor \tfrac{k}{2} \rfloor\}$. We define the isometry
\begin{equation}
    W_l=(V_l\otimes V_{k-l})V_k^\dagger:\ \text{Sym}^k(\C^D)\to\text{Sym}^l(\C^D)\otimes\text{Sym}^{k-l}(\C^D),
\end{equation}
so that $\Omega_k=(V_l\otimes V_{k-l})^\dagger\,W_l\Omega_k^{\rm sym}W_l^\dagger\,(V_l\otimes V_{k-l})$. Writing $T_l$ also for the transpose of the factor $\text{Sym}^l(\C^D)$ in the occupation basis, the partial transpose becomes
\begin{align}
    \Omega_k^{T_l} &= \left((V_l\otimes V_{k-l})^\dagger\,W_l\Omega_k^{\rm sym}W_l^\dagger\,(V_l\otimes V_{k-l})\right)^{T_l}\\
    &=\left(V_l^T\otimes V_{k-l}^\dagger\right)\,\left(W_l\Omega_k^{\rm sym}W_l^\dagger\right)^{T_l}\,\left(V_l^*\otimes V_{k-l}\right)\\
    &= (V_l\otimes V_{k-l})^\dagger\,\left(W_l\Omega_k^{\rm sym}W_l^\dagger\right)^{T_l}\,(V_l\otimes V_{k-l}),
\end{align}
since $V_l$ is real, so that $V_l^T=V_l^\dagger$ and $V_l^*=V_l$. Conjugating back with $V_l\otimes V_{k-l}$, the condition becomes
\begin{equation}
    \Omega_k^{T_l}\succeq0\iff\left(W_l\,\Omega_k^{\rm sym}W_l^\dagger\right)^{T_l}\succeq0,\qquad 1\leq l\leq\lfloor k/2\rfloor.
\end{equation}
This is the most expensive constraint of the SDP, since it encodes the state in the larger space $\text{Sym}^l(\C^D)\otimes\text{Sym}^{k-l}(\C^D)$ to support the partial transpose. While these constraints are not necessary for the hierarchy to be complete, they help convergence at finite $k$, as shown in Ref.~\cite{Navascues2009}.

\subsection{Projection onto the support of $\rho$}\label{app:supportReduction}

\begin{equation}

\begin{equation}

\end{equation}

\section{Linear program for non-polynomial functions}\label{app:LP}

\subsection{Construction of the LP and of the single SDP}
We optimize over symmetric polynomials of degree at most $k$. Since polynomials of lower degree can be lifted to degree $k$ (Lemma~\ref{lem:lifting}), we parametrize them as $P(\a)=\sum_{\lambda\vdash k}c_\lambda s_\lambda(\a)$, and the LP variables are the coefficients $\vec c=(c_\lambda)_\lambda$. Given the function $F$, we require $P(\a)\leq F(\a)$ for all spectra $\a$, and enforce this constraint discretely on a grid of spectra $\{\a^g\}_g$, where the values $s_\lambda(\a^g)$ and $F(\a^g)$ are precomputed. By symmetry, it suffices to take grid points with $\a_{g,1}\geq\a_{g,2}\geq\dots\geq\a_{g,d_A}\geq0$ and $\sum_i\a_{g,i}=1$.

Writing $\mu_\lambda(\Phi)=\tr\left((\tfrac{1}{f^\lambda}\Pi^\lambda\otimes\I^B)\Phi\right)$, the objective is $\tr\left((O_P\otimes\I^B)\Omega_k\right)=\sum_\lambda c_\lambda\,\mu_\lambda(\Omega_k)$, and therefore  the bound reads
\begin{equation}
    F_{{\rm LB}_k}=\max_{\vec c}\ \min_{\Omega_k}\ \sum_\lambda c_\lambda\,\mu_\lambda(\Omega_k)
    \quad\text{s.t.}\quad \sum_\lambda c_\lambda s_\lambda(\a^g)\leq F(\a^g)\ \ \forall g,
\end{equation}
with $\Omega_k$ ranging over the feasible set of Eq.~\eqref{eq:SDPcroof1}. Since the objective is bilinear, the set of feasible $\Omega_k$ is convex and compact, and the set of valid $\vec{c}$ is convex, we can exchange the maximum and the minimum~\cite{Sion1958}. Then, for fixed $\Omega_k$, the inner maximization is an LP whose dual is
\begin{equation}
    \min_{y\geq0}\ \sum_g y_g F(\a^g)\quad\text{s.t.}\quad \sum_g y_g\,s_\lambda(\a^g)=\mu_\lambda(\Omega_k)\ \ \forall\lambda .
\end{equation}
The primal is always feasible, since the constant polynomial $P=\min_g F(\a^g)$ is admissible, so strong duality holds. We merge the LP and the SDP to obtain a single SDP,
\begin{align}
    F_{{\rm LB}_k}=\min_{\Omega_k,\,y}\ & \sum_g y_g F(\a^g)\\
    \text{s.t. }& \Omega_k\ \text{feasible for Eq.~\eqref{eq:SDPcroof1}},\quad y\geq0, \nonumber\\
    & \sum_g y_g\,s_\lambda(\a^g)=\tr\left((\tfrac{1}{f^\lambda}\Pi^\lambda\otimes\I^B)\,\Omega_k\right)\quad\forall\lambda\vdash k. \nonumber
\end{align}
The coefficients $c_\lambda$ of the optimal polynomial are recovered as the dual variables of the equality constraints. Since the grid is discrete, the optimal polynomial $P^*$ may exceed $F$ between grid points. We therefore report $F_{{\rm LB}_k}-\epsilon$, where $\epsilon=\max_\a\left(P^*(\a)-F(\a)\right)$ is estimated from the polynomial returned by the solver. This is a valid lower bound, since $P^*-\epsilon\leq F$ on all spectra.

\subsection{Convergence of the bound}\label{app:LPconvergence}
Here we prove Result~\ref{thm:non-polyHier} for the bound $\widetilde F_{{\rm LB}_k}$ of Eq.~\eqref{eq:FLBexact}, in which $P\leq F$ is imposed on all spectra. Monotonicity follows naturally from the facts that admissible polynomials stay admissible at higher level by lifting (Lemma~\ref{lem:lifting}), and that the SDP is monotone (Appendix~\ref{app:monotonicity}).

By the Weierstrass approximation theorem, for any continuous function $F'$ and any $\eta>0$, we can find a polynomial $P'$ such that $|F'(\a)-P'(\a)| < \eta$ for all spectra $\a$. Then, by averaging over permutations of the variables, for any continuous symmetric function $F$, we can find a symmetric polynomial $P$ such that $|F(\a)-P(\a)| < \eta$ for all spectra $\a$.
Shifting $P$ by $\eta$, for any $F$ and any $\eta>0$, there exists a symmetric polynomial $P$ such that $P(\a) < F(\a)$ and $F(\a)-P(\a) < 2\eta$ for all spectra $\a$. This $P$ is admissible for $\deg P \leq k$, and therefore  by Result~\ref{thm:ConvergenceSDPcroof}, $\lim_{k\to\infty}\widetilde F_{{\rm LB}_k}(\rho)\geq\lim_{k\to\infty}P_{{\rm LB}_k}(\rho)=\widehat P(\rho)\geq\widehat F(\rho)-2\eta$. Conversely, $P\leq F$ implies $\widehat P(\rho)\leq\widehat F(\rho)$ for every admissible $P$, and therefore $\widetilde F_{{\rm LB}_k}(\rho)\leq\widehat F(\rho)$. Since $\eta$ is arbitrary,
\begin{equation}
    \lim_{k\to\infty}\widetilde F_{{\rm LB}_k}(\rho)=\widehat F(\rho).
\end{equation}

With a finite grid, the set of admissible polynomials is larger, and therefore $F_{{\rm LB}_k}\geq\widetilde F_{{\rm LB}_k}$, and $F_{{\rm LB}_k}-\epsilon\leq\widehat F(\rho)$, where $\epsilon$ is the grid error of the returned polynomial. The uncorrected value $F_{{\rm LB}_k}$ is nondecreasing in the level by the same argument as above, but the corrected value $F_{{\rm LB}_k}-\epsilon$ need not be, since the grid error can change between levels. In our numerical results, we observe that increasing the level yields tighter bounds.
\deeper{Sion's theorem needs one of the two sets to be compact; the set of feasible $\Omega_k$ is, but say so at each use. State the Weierstrass approximation on the simplex together with the symmetrisation step as a lemma, since both the non-polynomial result and the LOCC proof rely on it.}

\section{LOCC feasibility SDP}\label{app:LOCC}
\subsection{Construction of the SDP}
Let $\ket{\psi}$ be the initial pure state and $\rho$ the target mixed state. By Ref.~\cite{Jonathan_1999}, $\ket{\psi}$ can be converted into $\rho$ by LOCC if and only if there is a decomposition $\{p_i,\ket{\psi_i}\}$ of $\rho$ such that
\begin{equation}\label{eq:LOCCcriterion}
    \sum_i p_i E_j(\psi_i)\leq E_j(\psi)\qquad\text{for all } j=1,\dots,d_A-1 .
\end{equation}
Since all these inequalities must hold for the same decomposition, testing them one monotone at a time is not sufficient. Instead, we combine the LP construction of Appendix~\ref{app:LP} for all the Vidal monotones into a single SDP.

For each $j$, let $\mathcal{P}_j$ be the set of symmetric polynomials $P_j$ of degree at most $k$ that satisfy $P_j(\a^g)\leq E_j(\a^g)$ on all grid points, and let
\begin{equation}
    \epsilon_j(P_j)=\max_{\a}\left(P_j(\a)-E_j(\a)\right)
\end{equation}
be the maximal violation of $P_j$ between grid points. If a decomposition $\{p_i,\ket{\psi_i}\}$ satisfies Eq.~\eqref{eq:LOCCcriterion}, then its operator $\omega_{k}$ is feasible for Eq.~\eqref{eq:SDPcroof1} and, for every $P_j\in\mathcal{P}_j$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:LOCCnecessary}
\tr\left((O_{P_j}\otimes\I^B)\,\omega_{k}\right)=\sum_ip_iP_j(\a_i)\leq\sum_ip_iE_j(\a_i)+\epsilon_j(P_j)\leq E_j(\psi)+\epsilon_j(P_j),
\end{equation}
where $\a_i$ is the entanglement spectrum of $\ket{\psi_i}$. Therefore, if a certain decomposition can be transformed from $\ket{\psi}$ with LOCC, then it can violate the $j$'th inequality by at most the grid error $\epsilon_j(P_j)$ for each polynomial.

With the moments $\mu_\lambda(\Phi)=\tr\left((\tfrac{1}{f^\lambda}\Pi^\lambda\otimes\I^B)\Phi\right)$ of Appendix~\ref{app:LP}, the optimization of Eq.~\eqref{eq:LOCCminmaxt} is computed by the SDP
\begin{align}\label{eq:LOCCsdp}
    s_k=\min_{\Omega_k,\,y^{(j)},\,s}\ & s\\
    \text{s.t. }& \Omega_k\ \text{feasible for Eq.~\eqref{eq:SDPcroof1}},\quad y^{(j)}\geq0\ \ \forall j, \nonumber\\
    & \sum_g y^{(j)}_g\,s_\lambda(\a^g)=\mu_\lambda(\Omega_k)\quad\forall\lambda\vdash k,\ \forall j, \nonumber\\
    & \sum_g y^{(j)}_g\,E_j(\a^g)-E_j(\psi)\leq s\quad\forall j. \nonumber
\end{align}

For fixed $\Omega_k$, the minimization over each $y^{(j)}$ is the dual LP of Appendix~\ref{app:LP} with $F=E_j$, and therefore by strong duality
\begin{equation}\label{eq:LOCCminmax}
    s_k=\min_{\Omega_k}\ \max_{j}\ \max_{P_j\in\mathcal{P}_j}\Big(\tr\left((O_{P_j}\otimes\I^B) \Omega_k\right)-E_j(\psi)\Big).
\end{equation}
Thus $s_k$ is the smallest worst-case violation of Eq.~\eqref{eq:LOCCcriterion} over the relaxations of decompositions of $\rho$, where each violation is measured with the best grid-admissible polynomial. For a single monotone, $s_k=F_{{\rm LB}_k}-E_j(\psi)$ with $F=E_j$.

Let $t$ be a probability vector with $t_j \geq 0$ and $\sum_j t_j = 1$. Then,
\begin{align}\label{eq:LOCCminmaxT}
    s_k=\min_{\Omega_k} \max_t \max_{P_j\in\mathcal{P}_j}\ \sum_j t_j\Big(\tr\left((O_{P_j}\otimes\I^B)\Omega_k\right)-E_j(\psi)\Big).
\end{align}
With the substitution $Q_j=t_jP_j$, the constraint $P_j\in\mathcal{P}_j$ becomes $Q_j(\a^g)\leq t_jE_j(\a^g)$ for all $g$, and the objective is linear in $\Omega_k$ and in $(t,Q_j)$. The feasible set of $\Omega_k$ is convex and compact, and the set of $(t,Q_j)$ is convex. Again, here the minimum and the maximum can be exchanged by Ref.~\cite{Sion1958}:
\begin{align}
    s_k&=\min_{\Omega_k}\ \max_{t,\,Q_j}\ \sum_j \Big(\tr\left((O_{Q_j}\otimes\I^B)\Omega_k\right)-t_jE_j(\psi)\Big)\nonumber\\
    &=\max_{t,\,Q_j}\ \min_{\Omega_k}\ \sum_j \Big(\tr\left((O_{Q_j}\otimes\I^B)\Omega_k\right)-t_jE_j(\psi)\Big).\label{eq:LOCCmaxmin}
\end{align}
Let $t^*$ and $Q_j^*$ be the optimal vector $t$ and optimal polynomials, which are given by the dual variables of the SDP~\eqref{eq:LOCCsdp}; for $t^*_j>0$, $Q^*_j=t^*_jP^*_j$ with $P^*_j\in\mathcal{P}_j$.
By Eq.~\eqref{eq:LOCCmaxmin}, they satisfy
\begin{equation}\label{eq:LOCCcertificateIneq}
    \sum_j \Big(\tr\left((O_{Q^*_j}\otimes\I^B)\Omega_k\right)-t^*_jE_j(\psi)\Big)\geq s_k
\end{equation}
for every feasible $\Omega_k$.

Let $\epsilon_j=\max_\a\big(Q^*_j(\a)-t^*_jE_j(\a)\big)$, which equals $t^*_j\,\epsilon_j(P^*_j)$ for $t^*_j>0$. If $\ket{\psi}$ can be converted into $\rho$ by LOCC, some decomposition satisfies Eq.~\eqref{eq:LOCCcriterion}, and its $\omega_k$ is feasible. As in Eq.~\eqref{eq:LOCCnecessary}, $\tr\big((O_{Q^*_j}\otimes\I^B)\,\omega_k\big)=\sum_ip_iQ^*_j(\a_i) \leq t^*_jE_j(\psi)+\epsilon_j$, and therefore Eq.~\eqref{eq:LOCCcertificateIneq} gives
\begin{equation}
    s_k \leq \sum_j \Big(\tr\left((O_{Q^*_j}\otimes\I^B)\,\omega_k \right)-t^*_jE_j(\psi)\Big) \leq \sum_j \epsilon_j.
\end{equation}
Thus, if $s_k > \sum_j \epsilon_j$, $\ket{\psi}$ cannot be converted into $\rho$ by LOCC. If the condition does not hold, the test does not tell us anything, in which case, for $k\geq3$, we can extract the decomposition from $\Omega_k$ and repair it as in Sec.~\ref{sec:extractingTheDecomposition}, and if the criterion Eq.~\eqref{eq:LOCCcriterion} holds, the state can be converted into $\rho$ by LOCC.
In this procedure, the grid errors $\epsilon_j$ need to be computed in a postprocessing step from the polynomials returned by the solver.

\subsection{Detection at finite level}
We impose $P_j\leq E_j$ on all spectra.
Consider the decomposition $\{p_i, \dyad{\varphi_i}\}$ of $\rho$ that minimizes
\begin{equation}
    s^*=\max_t \sum_j t_j \left( \left(\sum_i p_i E_j(\varphi_i)\right)  - E_j(\psi) \right)
\end{equation}
over probability vectors $t$.
If $\ket{\psi}\overset{\text{LOCC}}{\nrightarrow}\rho$, at least one Vidal monotone $E_j$ will be exceeded by the decomposition and setting $t_j=1$, we get that $s^*>0$.
We know $\widetilde{s}_k \leq s^*$ since we are relaxing the set of feasible $\omega_k$.
By App.~\ref{app:ProofCongNonExtHier}, as $k\to\infty$, the few-copy marginals of $\Omega_k$ approach mixtures $\sum q_i\dyad{\phi_i}^{\otimes n}$ with $\{q_i, \dyad{\phi_i}\}$ a decomposition of $\rho$, and since by App.~\ref{app:LPconvergence} each $E_j$ can be approximated to arbitrary precision from below, $\widetilde{s}_k \to s^*$ as $k \to \infty$ and every non-convertible pair is eventually detected.

\subsection{Upper bounds by gradient descent}\label{app:HJW}
By the HJW theorem~\cite{HJW}, every decomposition of $\rho=\sum_i\lambda_i\dyad{e_i}$
into $M$ pure states is obtained
by mixing the vectors $\sqrt{\lambda_i}\ket{e_i}$ with an $M\times M$ unitary. Therefore every unitary provides a decomposition of
$\rho$ and hence an upper bound on the convex roof. As in Ref.~\cite{Rothlisberger2009}, we minimize this upper bound by
gradient descent over the unitaries, with $M=r^2$ and several random starting points, and keep the best value.
